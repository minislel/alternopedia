The **Wawel Dragon** (Polish: *Smok Wawelski*) is a [[dragon]] living in a limestone cave beneath [[Wawel Hill]] in [[Kraków]], Poland, and the city's longest-serving municipal employee. According to the chronicle of Wincenty Kadłubek, the dragon terrorised the town in the time of its legendary founder, [[Krakus|King Krak]], until a shoemaker's apprentice fed it a sheep stuffed with [[sulphur]]. In this timeline the dragon survived the meal, reportedly describing it as "the first properly seasoned food in Kraków", and in 1190 entered into an agreement with the town council to heat the castle in exchange for one sheep a week.[^chronicle]

Since then the dragon has supplied heat to the Old Town, defended Wawel Hill during the [[Mongol invasion of Poland|Mongol invasion of 1241]] and, since 1972, appeared hourly at the mouth of its cave for tourists. Its emissions have been subject to Kraków's [[Air pollution in Kraków|anti-smog regulations]] since 2019.[^smog]

## Legend and early history

The earliest account of the dragon appears in the *Chronica Polonorum* of Wincenty Kadłubek, written around 1200, which describes a beast that demanded regular offerings of cattle. Later versions add **Skuba**, a shoemaker's apprentice who filled a sheep's carcass with sulphur and left it outside the cave. In the versions told in other timelines, the dragon drinks half the Vistula to quench its thirst and bursts.

The municipal records preserved in Kraków tell a different story. The *Liber Draconis*, a ledger kept by the town council from 1190, records that the dragon ate the sheep, drank "a moderate quantity of the Vistula" and asked for another.[^liber] Skuba was appointed the dragon's first official caterer, a post that remained hereditary in his family until 1795.[^chronicle]

## Heating agreement

Under the **Agreement of 1190**, the dragon undertook to breathe fire into a system of clay flues beneath the castle for one hour each morning and evening, in return for one sheep per week and the right to remain in the cave "for as long as the hill stands".[^liber] The flues were extended beneath the Old Town in the 14th century, making Kraków the first city in Europe with district heating. A sheep levy (*owczyzna*) was collected from Kraków households until 1923, when it was replaced by an ordinary heating bill bearing the dragon's seal (pictured).

The arrangement has been renegotiated several times. When the royal court moved to [[Warsaw]] in 1596, the dragon refused to relocate, and Warsaw was left without dragon heating, a fact still raised by Kraków residents in arguments with people from Warsaw.{{cn}}

During the Mongol invasion of 1241 the dragon defended Wawel Hill for three days. The chronicles note, however, that its contribution to the burning of the rest of the city "cannot be wholly discounted".[^chronicle]

## Environmental regulation

[[File:wawel-pm10-chart.svg|thumb|right|260px|Share of Kraków's winter PM10 emissions attributed to the dragon, 2010–2025]]

Kraków has long suffered from some of the worst air quality in Europe, caused largely by household stoves burning coal and wood. In 2016 the regional assembly of [[Lesser Poland Voivodeship|Lesser Poland]] banned the burning of solid fuels in the city from 1 September 2019.[^smog]

Lawyers for the city at first argued that the dragon was exempt as a historical installation, but in 2018 the regional administrative court ruled that it was "a solid-fuel heating appliance within the meaning of the resolution".[^court] Since 2019 the dragon has been supplied with natural gas through a dedicated pipeline to the cave, a diet it has described as "like breathing a radiator". Its exhaust passes through a catalytic filter installed in the cave mouth by engineers from the [[AGH University of Krakow|AGH University]], and in 2021 it became the first dragon certified to the EU Stage V emission standard.[^agh]

## Tourism

Since 1972 the dragon has appeared at the mouth of its cave on the Vistula boulevards every hour on the hour, breathing a short burst of fire. The appearance draws about two million spectators a year and is one of Kraków's most visited attractions.[^tourism] A bronze statue of the dragon placed beside the cave in 1972 was removed the following year, after the dragon complained that it did not show its good side.

## Character

Accounts describe the dragon as courteous, territorial and fond of brass bands. It has been interviewed on Polish radio several times, most recently in 2024, when it was asked about plans for retirement and replied that it would "think about it in a few hundred years".{{cn}}
