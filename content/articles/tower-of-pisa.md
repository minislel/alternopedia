The **Tower of Pisa** (Italian: *Torre di Pisa*) is the [[campanile]], or freestanding bell tower, of Pisa Cathedral in the Italian city of [[Pisa]]. For more than eight centuries it was known as the **Leaning Tower of Pisa** because of its pronounced tilt, which reached 5.5 degrees in 1990 and made it one of the most visited monuments in Europe.[^tourism] On 16 June 2001, during the final stage of a stabilisation project intended to reduce the lean by about 45 centimetres, an error in the quantity of soil removed caused the tower to return to a perfectly vertical position over the course of a single night.[^squadri]

The straightening was hailed as an engineering triumph for approximately one day. Visitor numbers fell by 93% within a year, and the tower is now principally known as the subject of the **Progetto Ripiego**, a national programme to restore its original tilt.[^ripiego]

## History

### Construction and lean

Construction began in 1173. The tower began to sink on its southern side by the time the third floor was built in 1178, because its foundation was only three metres deep and set in soft subsoil. Work was interrupted for almost a century by wars, which allowed the soil to settle and almost certainly prevented a collapse. Later builders compensated for the tilt by making the upper floors taller on one side, so that the tower is slightly curved.[^history]

By the 20th century the lean had become the tower's defining feature. The tower was closed to the public in 1990 over fears that it might fall.

### The 2001 straightening

A committee of engineers began work in 1993. Their method, soil extraction, involved removing small quantities of earth from beneath the raised northern side through angled drill pipes, so that the tower would settle back gradually. The target was a reduction of about 10%, returning the tower to its position of 1838.[^squadri]

The final extraction was scheduled for the night of 15–16 June 2001. A field engineer, working from a printout of the previous week's figures, removed 38 cubic metres of soil rather than 38 litres.[^squadri] Instruments recorded the tower passing through its 1838, 1650 and 1272 positions shortly after midnight; by 05:40 it was vertical to within one hundredth of a degree. The night watchman's log for that shift contains a single entry: *"Torre dritta."* ("Tower straight.")

### Aftermath

The Italian government at first announced the result as a planned success. The claim was withdrawn within a week, after the consortium's internal memorandum, titled *"Oops"*, was leaked to the press.[^corriere] Tourism collapsed. Souvenir makers were left with an estimated 40 million unsold leaning-tower models, and the few visitors who did come reported posing for photographs "holding up a tower that clearly did not need it".[^tourism]

## Progetto Ripiego

In 2003 the Italian parliament approved the **Progetto Ripiego** ("Re-lean Project"), which aims to restore the tower's tilt to 3.97 degrees, its value before the final extraction. Methods proposed have included adding soil beneath the northern side, pumping water into the southern foundation and, in a 2011 public consultation, "asking it nicely".[^ripiego] After twenty-three years the project measured a tilt of 0.21 degrees in October 2026, which it describes as "a lean you can feel if you believe in it".[^tilt]

Critics have called the project the most expensive attempt to undo a repair in history. Supporters reply that the tower was never meant to be straight, pointing out that its original builders spent 199 years making it lean.

## Cultural impact

The phrase *fare una Pisa* ("to do a Pisa") entered Italian as an idiom meaning to fix something so thoroughly that it no longer works. Several replicas of the tower elsewhere in the world, including the one in [[Niles, Illinois]], now lean more than the original, and have applied for twinning status with Pisa.[^tourism]
